# Thyroid

Applies to both sexes. Thyroid disease is considerably more common in women; the thyroid
in pregnancy is treated separately.

## Key Markers

- **TSH:** normal ~0.4–4.0 mIU/L. A "comfortable" guide for many is 1.0–2.5, but this is
  not a strict rule: the upper limit of normal rises with age, and in the elderly a
  slightly elevated TSH may be a normal variant. TSH is the most sensitive screening
  test.
- **Free T4 (fT4):** ~0.8–1.8 ng/dL (10–23 pmol/L).
- **Free T3 (fT3):** ~2.3–4.2 pg/mL (3.5–6.5 pmol/L).
- **TPO antibodies (anti-TPO):** usually < 35 IU/mL. A marker of an autoimmune process
  (Hashimoto's, less often Graves').
- **Thyroglobulin antibodies (anti-Tg):** usually < 40 IU/mL.
- **TSH-receptor antibodies (TRAb):** a marker of Graves' disease.

**Important about method:** biotin (often in hair/nail supplements, doses of 5–10 mg)
distorts many immunoassays – it can falsely lower TSH and raise thyroid hormones,
mimicking hyperthyroidism. Stop biotin 2–3 days before testing. TSH is also affected by
acute illness, sleep loss, time of day (slightly higher at night/early morning),
estrogens, and oral contraceptives.

## Hypothyroidism

**Overt hypothyroidism:** TSH elevated, fT4 reduced.
**Subclinical hypothyroidism:** TSH elevated, fT4 normal.

Symptoms: fatigue, weight gain or difficulty losing weight, cold intolerance, dry skin,
hair loss, constipation, puffiness, slowed pace, low mood, "brain fog"; in women – cycle
disturbances, heavy menstruation, fertility problems; bradycardia.

Approach:
- Confirm persistence (retest TSH + fT4 in ~6–8 weeks; a single elevated TSH often
  normalizes, especially after illness or stress).
- **Check anti-TPO** – if elevated, the cause is most often autoimmune Hashimoto's
  thyroiditis (the most common cause of hypothyroidism in iodine-sufficient regions).
- Lifestyle and nutrition (may be recommended): adequate but not excessive iodine
  (~150 µg/day for adults; pregnant women need more – see below; iodine excess is harmful
  and can worsen Hashimoto's); selenium 100–200 µg/day (the data on benefit in
  Hashimoto's are moderately positive); adequate iron/ferritin, vitamin D, zinc – their
  deficiency worsens thyroid function.
- In Hashimoto's, a gluten-free diet does not help everyone; it makes sense to discuss it
  with co-existing celiac disease or intolerance. The main focus is reducing systemic
  inflammation, normalizing weight, and sleep.
- **Levothyroxine (thyroxine) is prescribed only by a physician.** The skill does not
  prescribe or adjust the dose. The decision to treat subclinical hypothyroidism depends
  on the TSH level, antibodies, symptoms, age, and pregnancy – this is a medical
  decision.

## Hyperthyroidism / Thyrotoxicosis

TSH reduced (often suppressed), fT4 and/or fT3 elevated.

Symptoms: palpitations, tachycardia, anxiety, tremor, sweating, weight loss with a
preserved appetite, heat intolerance, frequent stools, sleep disturbances; in women –
cycle disturbances; in Graves' disease, eye symptoms are possible (exophthalmos).

This condition requires mandatory and fairly urgent medical attention – especially with
marked tachycardia, arrhythmia, or significant weight loss. The most common cause is
Graves' disease (autoimmune); also toxic nodular goiter and thyroiditis. Thyroid storm
is an emergency. Here the skill does not offer "lifestyle optimization" as the basis – it
refers to a physician.

## Thyroid Nodules

Nodules are very common and are benign in the majority of cases. Management (ultrasound
risk stratification, the need for a biopsy) is determined by a physician. Warning signs:
rapid nodule growth, a hard fixed nodule, hoarseness, enlarged cervical lymph nodes,
difficulty swallowing or breathing – an in-person exam without delay.

## Ratios and Interpretation Nuances

- **Low fT3 with normal TSH and fT4** can occur in non-thyroidal illness syndrome
  ("low T3 syndrome") – against a background of illness, fasting, severe stress, or a
  harsh caloric deficit. This is an adaptive response, not a primary thyroid disease;
  the cause should be treated, not "T3 added."
- **Both TSH and fT4 low** – central (pituitary) hypothyroidism is possible; a rare but
  important situation that requires evaluation of the pituitary axis.
- Isolated elevated anti-TPO with normal TSH and fT4 is an increased risk of future
  hypothyroidism, but not yet a disease; periodic TSH monitoring is needed.

## Thyroid and Pregnancy (Only Under a Physician's Supervision)

This is an area of heightened responsibility – here the skill is especially restrained
and refers to a physician:
- Iodine requirements during pregnancy and lactation are higher (~220–290 µg/day); many
  women are advised to take iodine within prenatal complexes – but the dosage is
  determined by a physician.
- TSH reference ranges in pregnancy differ from the general-population ones and depend on
  the trimester; a "normal" non-pregnant TSH may be suboptimal for pregnancy.
- Untreated hypothyroidism in pregnancy is linked to risks for mother and fetus.
- A woman with thyroid disease who is planning pregnancy or is pregnant needs a
  physician's supervision and, as a rule, more frequent monitoring – the skill emphasizes
  this but does not prescribe doses or management.

## When to Refer Firmly to a Physician
- Signs of thyrotoxicosis (suppressed TSH, high hormones, palpitations, weight loss).
- Marked hypothyroidism (significantly elevated TSH, low fT4) with symptoms.
- A nodule with warning signs; a rapidly growing goiter; hoarseness, swallowing problems.
- Any thyroid questions in pregnancy, when planning pregnancy, or during lactation.
- Positive antibodies with a progressively rising TSH.
- Eye symptoms (bulging eyes, double vision) against a background of thyroid disease.
