# Pituitary, Prolactin, GH/IGF-1, Melatonin

## Prolactin

A pituitary hormone; important in both sexes, not only in breastfeeding women.

**Reference ranges (approximate, method-dependent):**
- Women (non-pregnant): ~4–30 ng/mL.
- Men: ~3–18 ng/mL.
- In pregnancy and lactation it is physiologically high.

**The conditions of the test are critical.** Prolactin rises with stress, physical
activity, sex, breast stimulation, food intake, sleep loss, even the venipuncture itself.
Test in the morning, fasting, in a calm state, ideally ~2–3 hours after waking, not right
after stress. A once-off moderately elevated prolactin often normalizes on a retest under
correct conditions – this should be explained so the user is not alarmed.

**Causes of elevated prolactin:**
- Physiological: stress, exercise, sleep, food, pregnancy/lactation.
- Medications – a very common cause: many antipsychotics, some antidepressants,
  metoclopramide, domperidone, verapamil, estrogens, opioids. Always clarify medications.
- Hypothyroidism (elevated TRH stimulates prolactin) – therefore TSH is always checked
  with hyperprolactinemia.
- Prolactinoma (pituitary adenoma) – with persistently and significantly elevated
  prolactin.
- Macroprolactin – a laboratory phenomenon: a biologically inactive form gives a falsely
  high result. With an asymptomatic elevation it makes sense to ask the lab to check for
  macroprolactin.

**Symptoms of prolactin excess:** in women – cycle disturbance/cessation, galactorrhea,
infertility; in men – reduced libido, erectile dysfunction, lowered testosterone
(prolactin suppresses the axis), sometimes galactorrhea, gynecomastia. Persistently high
prolactin + headaches + visual disturbances is a warning sign requiring pituitary
imaging.

This is largely a medical domain: the skill explains mechanisms, draws attention to the
test conditions and medications, recommends retesting correctly, and with a persistent
significant elevation refers to an endocrinologist. The skill does not recommend dopamine
agonists.

## The Pituitary Axis – General Principle

The pituitary controls the peripheral glands through tropic hormones. When interpreting,
always look at the pair "central hormone + peripheral":
- TSH ↔ thyroid (fT4, fT3)
- LH/FSH ↔ gonads (testosterone / estradiol, progesterone)
- ACTH ↔ adrenals (cortisol)
- GH ↔ IGF-1

This gives the answer "primary problem or central":
- A low peripheral hormone + a HIGH tropic hormone → a problem in the gland itself
  (primary).
- A low peripheral hormone + a LOW or inappropriately normal tropic hormone → a problem
  at the level of the pituitary/hypothalamus (central, secondary).

A combination of deficiency across several axes (for example, low thyroid and sex
hormones together with low tropic hormones) is a reason to consider hypopituitarism and
to evaluate the pituitary. This is a referral to a physician.

## GH (Growth Hormone) and IGF-1

- GH itself is secreted in pulses (peaks in deep sleep and after intense exercise), so a
  single GH measurement is of little value.
- **IGF-1** is a stable integral marker; the reference range depends on age and sex (the
  adult guide is wide and declines with age). Interpret only against an age-specific
  reference range.
- Natural GH stimuli: deep sleep, intense training, a period without food, heat stress
  (sauna).
- Suppressed by: chronic sugar excess and high insulin, sleep loss, obesity.
- Very high IGF-1 in an adult is a reason to rule out acromegaly (changes in facial
  features, enlarging hands/feet, sweating) – a referral to a physician. Very low – GH
  deficiency is possible.
- The skill does NOT recommend growth hormone, GH-releasing peptides, or secretagogues –
  this is a prescription domain. It can recommend only natural stimuli (sleep, training).

## Melatonin and Circadian Rhythm

Melatonin is the hormone of darkness, a marker of biological night. It is more a
"conductor" of the circadian rhythm than a sleeping pill.
- Natural secretion builds with the onset of darkness, peaks around 2–4 AM, and falls
  toward morning.
- Bright light, especially of the blue spectrum (screens), in the evening suppresses
  melatonin and shifts the rhythm.
- **Melatonin as a supplement:** low doses of 0.5–3 mg, 30–60 minutes before sleep; for
  correcting the rhythm (jet lag, a shifted sleep phase) the timing of the dose matters
  more than the dose. Start with low doses. It is an aid, not a substitute for sleep
  hygiene. For children and adolescents, and in pregnancy – only with a physician's
  agreement.
- **More important than supplements** is circadian hygiene: a stable sleep and wake time;
  bright light in the morning (10–30 minutes); dimmed light and fewer screens in the
  evening; a dark bedroom; avoiding caffeine in the second half of the day.

## When to Refer Firmly to a Physician
- Persistently and significantly elevated prolactin, especially with headaches or visual
  disturbances – pituitary imaging is needed.
- Galactorrhea outside pregnancy and lactation.
- Signs of hypopituitarism (deficiency across several hormonal axes simultaneously).
- Suspected acromegaly (changes in facial features, growth of hands/feet, rings/shoes
  becoming tight) or GH deficiency.
- Any space-occupying pituitary lesions on imaging.
