# Critical Values and Red Flags

This file is about situations where the skill stops "lifestyle optimization" and firmly
refers to a physician, and with emergency signs – to emergency care. When in doubt,
always err toward a referral to a physician.

## Emergencies (Emergency Care / Urgent Physician Visit the Same Day)

- **Signs of diabetic ketoacidosis:** very high glucose, nausea, vomiting, an
  acetone-like breath odor, frequent deep breathing, confusion, marked weakness.
- **Severe hypoglycemia:** confusion, impaired consciousness, seizures, profuse sweating
  and trembling – especially in people on glucose-lowering therapy.
- **Thyroid storm:** high fever, marked tachycardia/arrhythmia, agitation or confusion
  against a background of hyperthyroidism.
- **Adrenal crisis:** sudden weakness, vomiting, abdominal pain, confusion, a marked drop
  in blood pressure – especially in people with adrenal insufficiency or those taking /
  abruptly stopping glucocorticoids.
- **Pituitary apoplexy:** a sudden, severe headache with visual disturbance.
- Any **bleeding in postmenopause** – not an emergency as a threat to life, but it
  requires a mandatory in-person evaluation without delay.

In such cases the skill does not analyze labs or reason about lifestyle – it states
directly and calmly that the situation requires immediate medical care.

## Lab Values Requiring a Physician (Not an Emergency, but Without Delay)

**Carbohydrate metabolism:**
- Fasting glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or HbA1c ≥ 6.5% – probable diabetes.
- Fasting glucose persistently ≥ 11 mmol/L – urgently.
- Prediabetic values – a reason to see a physician and intervene actively.

**Thyroid:**
- TSH significantly elevated (a guide of > 10 mIU/L) or significantly reduced/suppressed.
- A suppressed TSH with high fT4/fT3 (thyrotoxicosis).
- Both TSH and fT4 low (suspected central hypothyroidism).

**Sex hormones:**
- Very high testosterone in a man not taking medication.
- Signs of hyperandrogenism in a woman with rapid progression or virilization.
- A menopausal picture (high FSH, amenorrhea) in a woman younger than 40 – POI.
- Low testosterone / estradiol with low LH/FSH + elevated prolactin.

**Prolactin:**
- Persistently and significantly elevated prolactin (especially manyfold above normal),
  all the more so with headaches or visual disturbance.

**Cortisol:**
- Signs of hypercortisolism in labs (a disrupted rhythm, high late-night cortisol).
- Low cortisol with a clinical picture of adrenal insufficiency.

**Pituitary:**
- Deficiency across several hormonal axes at once – suspected hypopituitarism.
- Very high IGF-1 (acromegaly) or very low.

## Clinical Red Flags (Even With "Normal" Labs)

- Unexplained weight loss, especially rapid.
- Space-occupying lesions, rapidly growing nodules (thyroid, breast, testes).
- Galactorrhea outside pregnancy/lactation.
- Visual disturbances, loss of visual fields, persistent new headaches (pituitary).
- Virilization in a woman (voice deepening, clitoromegaly, rapid hirsutism).
- Bleeding in postmenopause; heavy/intermenstrual bleeding.
- Amenorrhea of unclear cause (after pregnancy is excluded).
- Symptoms clearly not explained by the available labs – a reason not to "reassure with
  numbers" but to recommend further workup.

## Particularly Sensitive Groups – Heightened Caution

- **Pregnant and breastfeeding women:** any hormonal questions, nutraceuticals, and doses
  – coordinate with a physician. The skill gives only general principles and a referral.
- **Children and adolescents:** reference ranges and management are fundamentally
  different; the skill does not interpret with "adult" norms and refers to a pediatric
  endocrinologist.
- **People with eating disorders** (including in their history): do not give firm
  numerical prescriptions on calories and weight, do not recommend fasting or restrictive
  protocols; be especially gentle; with signs of an eating disorder, refer kindly to a
  specialist.
- **The elderly:** reference ranges shift (for example, the upper limit of TSH); the
  "optima" of younger age should not be applied to them mechanically.
- **People on hormonal or other significant therapy:** do not suggest discontinuation or
  self-adjustment; any changes go through the treating physician.

## Tone When Referring to a Physician

When referring to a physician, the skill keeps a calm, supportive tone: it does not
escalate anxiety, explains why exactly an in-person specialist is needed, and frames it
as a normal, reasonable next step. The goal is for the person to seek help, not to be
frightened.
