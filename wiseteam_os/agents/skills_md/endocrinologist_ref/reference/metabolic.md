# Metabolic Health: Insulin, Glucose, Diabetes

The principles apply to both sexes. Account for sex-specific factors: in women, insulin
resistance is closely linked to PCOS (see `female_health.md`); gestational diabetes is a
separate topic, managed only by a physician.

## Key Markers

- **Fasting glucose:** normal 3.9–5.5 mmol/L (70–99 mg/dL); a marker of good health
  < 5.0.
- **Fasting insulin:** ~2–25 µIU/mL; a marker of good health < 8 (metabolically healthy
  people are often lower). High insulin with normal glucose is an early sign of
  resistance.
- **HbA1c (glycated hemoglobin):** normal < 5.7%; a marker of good health < 5.3%.
  Reflects average blood sugar over ~3 months. Distorted by anemia, hemoglobinopathies,
  and recent blood loss – in these cases it is less reliable.
- **HOMA-IR** – a calculated index of insulin resistance.

### The HOMA-IR formula – mind the units
- If glucose is in **mmol/L**: HOMA-IR = (glucose × insulin) / 22.5
- If glucose is in **mg/dL**: HOMA-IR = (glucose × insulin) / 405
- Insulin is in µIU/mL (mIU/L) in both cases.

**Interpreting HOMA-IR (thresholds depend on the population and the insulin assay):**
- < 1.5 – good insulin sensitivity.
- 1.5–2.5 – a "gray zone," borderline values; view in the context of weight, waist, and
  symptoms.
- \> 2.5 – indicates insulin resistance.

Do not make a diagnosis out of a single HOMA-IR: insulin is a labile marker and the
measurement method has a strong influence. This is a screening tool, not a verdict. For
borderline values, retest strictly fasting.

## Stages of Impaired Glucose Metabolism

1. **Insulin resistance (compensated):** glucose and HbA1c are still normal, but insulin
   and HOMA-IR are elevated. The pancreas "overpays" with insulin, holding the sugar
   down. The earliest and most reversible stage – intervention is most effective here.
2. **Prediabetes:** fasting glucose 5.6–6.9 mmol/L (100–125 mg/dL) OR HbA1c 5.7–6.4% OR
   2-hour glucose on an OGTT of 7.8–11.0 mmol/L.
3. **Type 2 diabetes:** fasting glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L OR HbA1c ≥ 6.5% OR 2-hour glucose on
   an OGTT ≥ 11.1 mmol/L (the diagnosis is made by a physician, usually on two
   measurements or one with clear symptoms).

With newly detected hyperglycemia it is important not to miss type 1 diabetes and LADA
(latent autoimmune diabetes in adults) – especially in people without obesity, with
rapid weight loss, thirst, and marked symptoms. This requires urgent medical attention.

## Metabolic Syndrome

A combination of factors that sharply raises cardiometabolic risk. Criteria (≥ 3
present):
- Waist circumference: elevated (thresholds depend on sex and ethnic group; a guide is
  men ≥ 94–102 cm, women ≥ 80–88 cm).
- Triglycerides ≥ 1.7 mmol/L.
- HDL low: men < 1.0 mmol/L, women < 1.3 mmol/L.
- Blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg.
- Fasting glucose ≥ 5.6 mmol/L.

Visceral (intra-abdominal) fat is metabolically active and harmful; waist circumference
is a simple and strong marker. Pairing with the `cardiologist` and `nutritionist` skills
is appropriate here.

## Recommendations for Insulin Resistance (Lifestyle – First Line)

Evidence-based and may be recommended:
- **Reducing refined carbohydrate and sugar**, sugar-sweetened beverages. Emphasis on
  whole foods, slow carbohydrates.
- **Fiber 30+ g/day** – soluble fiber especially (vegetables, legumes, oats, seeds).
- **Adequate protein** at every meal – reduces the glycemic response, preserves muscle.
- **Physical activity:** strength training builds muscle – the main "warehouse" for
  glucose; even a short walk after a meal noticeably lowers postprandial sugar. A
  combination of strength and aerobic training is optimal.
- **Weight loss** when overweight – the most powerful lever; even a 5–10% reduction
  produces a substantial metabolic effect.
- **Sleep:** sleep loss and poor sleep quality reduce insulin sensitivity; sleep apnea
  worsens resistance – diagnose and treat.
- **Stress management:** chronically high cortisol raises glucose.
- **Meal sequencing:** eating vegetables and protein before carbohydrates within a meal
  lowers the glycemic peak.

Nutraceuticals (an addition, not a substitute for lifestyle; coordinate with a physician
when on concurrent therapy and in pregnancy):
- **Berberine** ~500 mg 2–3 times/day – noticeably improves glycemic markers; it has
  drug interactions (affects CYP enzymes), so when taking other medications, discuss with
  a physician.
- **Magnesium** for deficiency – linked to insulin sensitivity.
- **Alpha-lipoic acid** 300–600 mg/day.
- **Inositol** – especially with PCOS-associated resistance (see `female_health.md`).
- **Chromium** – the data are weak and contradictory; not a priority.
- **Intermittent fasting / a restricted eating window** may help some people, but it does
  not suit everyone; it is contraindicated in eating disorders and is not recommended
  without supervision for pregnant women, adolescents, or people with diabetes on
  glucose-lowering therapy (risk of hypoglycemia).

Metformin, GLP-1 agonists, and other medications – only as prescribed by a physician.

## When to Refer Firmly to a Physician
- Fasting glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or HbA1c ≥ 6.5% – probable diabetes, a physician is
  needed.
- Symptoms of hyperglycemia: intense thirst, frequent urination, unexplained weight loss,
  marked fatigue, blurred vision – do not delay seeking care.
- Fasting glucose ≥ 11 mmol/L, any signs of ketoacidosis (nausea, vomiting, an
  acetone-like odor, confusion, frequent deep breathing) – an emergency.
- Suspected type 1 diabetes / LADA (young age, no obesity, rapid onset).
- Episodes of hypoglycemia (trembling, sweating, confusion, hunger, especially in
  non-diabetics).
- Pregnancy with any glucose disturbances – gestational diabetes is managed by a
  physician.
