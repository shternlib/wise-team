# Female Hormonal Health

> Fundamental rule: in a woman of reproductive age, sex hormones are interpreted ONLY in
> relation to the day of the menstrual cycle. Without the cycle day, analysis of
> estradiol, progesterone, LH, and FSH is impossible. Always clarify the cycle day, its
> regularity and length, pregnancy/lactation, and use of oral contraceptives or MHT.
> Day 1 is the first day of menstruation.

## The Menstrual Cycle: Phases and Hormones

A normal cycle is 24–38 days (the classic "28" is just the midpoint). It is divided into
phases:

**Follicular phase (day 1 – ovulation):**
- Estradiol rises as the follicle matures.
- FSH is moderately elevated early, then declines.
- Progesterone is low.
- Testing sex hormones "on days 2–5 of the cycle" is the baseline assessment of ovarian
  reserve and the axis (FSH, LH, estradiol, and if needed AMH, prolactin, androgens).

**Ovulation (mid-cycle):**
- A sharp LH surge (and a smaller FSH surge) triggers release of the egg.
- Peak estradiol before ovulation.

**Luteal phase (ovulation – menstruation):**
- The corpus luteum produces progesterone – the peak is about 7 days after ovulation.
- Progesterone "on day 21" (more precisely, the mid-luteal phase) confirms that ovulation
  occurred. With a cycle longer/shorter than 28 days the test day shifts: the guide is
  ~7 days before the expected period.
- If conception does not occur, progesterone and estradiol fall and menstruation begins.

### Approximate reference ranges by phase (method-dependent, verify with the lab)
- **Estradiol:** early follicular ~20–60 pg/mL; periovulatory peak ~130–370 pg/mL;
  luteal ~50–230 pg/mL; postmenopause usually < 20–30 pg/mL.
- **Progesterone:** follicular < 1 ng/mL; a mid-luteal value > 3–5 ng/mL confirms
  ovulation (often 5–20 ng/mL).
- **FSH:** early follicular ~3–10 IU/L. FSH > 10–12 on days 2–3 signals declining
  ovarian reserve; persistently high FSH + low estradiol + amenorrhea is the picture of
  menopause/ovarian insufficiency.
- **LH:** early follicular ~2–10 IU/L; the ovulatory peak is many times higher.
- **LH/FSH ratio:** normally around 1. A ratio ≥ 2 in the early follicular phase occurs
  in PCOS (but this is a supportive, not diagnostic, sign).

## Androgens in Women

The female body needs androgens (libido, muscle, bone, mood), but in small amounts. They
are assessed via:
- **Total and free testosterone** (levels in women are many times lower than in men; a
  sensitive assay is needed).
- **SHBG** – low SHBG raises free (active) testosterone even with normal total T; low
  SHBG is typical of insulin resistance and often accompanies PCOS.
- **DHEA-S** – a predominantly adrenal androgen.
- **17-OH-progesterone** – to rule out non-classic congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH);
  taken in the morning in the early follicular phase.
- **Androstenedione.**

Signs of androgen excess (hyperandrogenism): hirsutism (male-pattern hair growth), acne,
androgenetic hair loss, irregular cycle. Abrupt onset, rapid progression, or virilization
(voice deepening, clitoromegaly) is a warning sign requiring urgent exclusion of an
androgen-producing tumor.

## PCOS (Polycystic Ovary Syndrome)

The most common endocrine disorder in women of reproductive age.

**Diagnostic criteria (Rotterdam, as updated in the 2023 international guideline):**
the diagnosis is made when 2 of 3 features are present after other causes are excluded:
1. Clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism.
2. Ovulatory dysfunction (irregular or absent cycles).
3. Polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound – or, per the 2023 guideline, elevated AMH
   as an alternative to ultrasound in adults.

Key clarifications:
- If both an irregular cycle and hyperandrogenism are present, ultrasound and AMH are not
  needed for the diagnosis.
- In adolescents, ultrasound and AMH cannot be used; a combination of an irregular cycle
  and hyperandrogenism is required, since polycystic morphology is commonly normal in
  adolescence.
- Before a PCOS diagnosis, the following must be excluded: thyroid disease,
  hyperprolactinemia, non-classic CAH (17-OH-progesterone), and where suspected, Cushing's
  syndrome and androgen-producing tumors.
- The diagnosis is made by a physician. The skill may state that the picture is
  consistent with the criteria and recommend evaluation.

**The metabolic side of PCOS:** insulin resistance is a common driver (even in lean women
with PCOS). Everyone with PCOS should be assessed for cardiometabolic risk: glucose,
insulin/HOMA-IR or HbA1c, lipids, blood pressure, waist circumference.

**Lifestyle – first-line therapy (may be recommended):**
- Weight loss when overweight (even 5–10% substantially improves ovulation and the
  androgen profile).
- A diet focused on insulin sensitivity: less refined carbohydrate and sugar, adequate
  fiber and protein, a Mediterranean pattern.
- Regular physical activity, strength training (muscles utilize glucose).
- Sleep and stress management.
- **Inositol** (myo-inositol, often combined with D-chiro-inositol at ~40:1) – a
  nutraceutical with a decent evidence base in PCOS: it improves insulin sensitivity and
  ovulatory function. Typically ~2–4 g of myo-inositol/day. It may be recommended, but
  when conception is planned, coordinate with a physician.
- Vitamin D for deficiency, omega-3.
- Metformin, oral contraceptives, and ovulation-induction agents – only as prescribed by
  a physician; the skill does not prescribe them.

## Perimenopause and Menopause

**Perimenopause** is the transition period (often from ~40–47 years, lasting on average
~4 years but variable) when cycles become irregular and hormones fluctuate. Characteristic
features: fluctuations (not just a decline) of estradiol, shortening or lengthening of
cycles, hot flashes, sleep disturbances, mood swings, anxiety, reduced libido, vaginal
dryness, and changes in cycle and bleeding heaviness.

**Important about labs in perimenopause:** hormones (FSH, estradiol) swing strongly from
month to month during this period, so a single test is of little value and a normal FSH
does not rule out perimenopause. A diagnosis of perimenopause in a woman aged 45+ with
typical symptoms is primarily clinical, based on the picture, not on a single result.
This is important to explain so the user does not draw false conclusions from one
"normal" result.

**Menopause** is a retrospective diagnosis: 12 consecutive months without menstruation
(in the absence of other causes). The average age is ~51 years. Hormonally: persistently
high FSH (often > 25–40 IU/L), low estradiol (< 20–30 pg/mL).

**Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI)** – cessation of ovarian function before age 40.
A menopausal picture (high FSH, amenorrhea) in a woman younger than 40 is NOT normal and
requires mandatory evaluation by a physician (causes, risks to bones and heart, the
question of therapy).

**What can be recommended on lifestyle in peri/postmenopause:**
- Strength training and weight-bearing activity – critical for bone (osteoporosis
  prevention) and muscle mass.
- Adequate protein (a guide of ~1.2–1.6 g/kg), calcium primarily from food, vitamin D to
  the target level.
- Sleep hygiene, limiting alcohol and caffeine (hot-flash triggers), stress management.
- Control of cardiometabolic risk: after menopause, risk rises for the heart, blood
  pressure, lipids, and visceral fat – this is a period of active prevention.
- Cognitive-behavioral techniques and breathing practices help with hot flashes and
  sleep.
- **MHT/HRT** (menopausal hormone therapy) is discussed with a physician on an individual
  basis (the balance of benefits and risks depends on age, time since menopause, and risk
  factors). The skill does not prescribe or discontinue MHT, but it may explain that this
  is a discussable option and recommend a consultation.

## PMS and PMDD

PMS is cyclical physical and emotional symptoms in the luteal phase that resolve with the
onset of menstruation. PMDD (premenstrual dysphoric disorder) is a severe form with
marked affective symptoms that affect daily life. They are linked not to a "hormone
deficiency" but to sensitivity to normal fluctuations of progesterone/allopregnanolone.
What can be recommended: regular exercise, stable sleep, limiting alcohol/salt/caffeine
in the luteal phase; there is an evidence base for calcium (~1,000–1,200 mg/day) and
moderate evidence for magnesium and vitamin B6 (do not exceed ~50–100 mg/day of B6 due to
the neurotoxicity of high doses). Marked PMDD is a reason to see a physician (effective
treatments are available).

## Amenorrhea and Irregular Cycles

Absence of menstruation or cycle disturbances always require working out the cause:
- **Pregnancy** – rule out first.
- **Functional hypothalamic amenorrhea** – a common cause in active, lean women: an
  energy deficit (too few calories relative to expenditure), intense training, stress,
  low weight. Hormonally: low LH/FSH, low estradiol. This is serious: a threat to bone
  and heart (a component of RED-S / the "athlete triad"). The basis of correction is
  restoring energy balance and reducing training load; a physician is needed.
- **PCOS** – see above.
- **Hyperprolactinemia, thyroid disease** – always check TSH and prolactin with any cycle
  disturbance.
- **Peri/postmenopause** – in women of the relevant age.

## Female Fertility and AMH

**AMH (anti-Mullerian hormone)** is a marker of ovarian reserve (the number of remaining
follicles), NOT a marker of egg "quality" and it does not predict the chance of natural
conception in the current cycle. It declines with age. It does not depend on the cycle
day (can be tested on any day) but is distorted by oral contraceptive use (lowers it) and
by biotin.

Approximate values (method-dependent, units ng/mL; verify with the lab and age):
- The decline of AMH with age is continuous; the median falls noticeably after age 35.
- AMH < ~1.0–1.2 ng/mL is usually interpreted as diminished ovarian reserve.
- High AMH often accompanies PCOS.
- AMH is always interpreted together with age and, when assessing fertility, with the
  antral follicle count on ultrasound.

It is important to explain correctly: low AMH means a smaller reserve and a smaller
"window," but not infertility; high AMH does not guarantee easy conception. Decisions on
fertility and IVF are made with a reproductive endocrinologist.

A baseline fertility assessment includes: confirmation of ovulation (mid-luteal
progesterone), FSH/LH/estradiol on days 2–5, AMH, TSH, prolactin, and ultrasound where
needed. Fertility is a couple's topic: when planning, it is important to assess the
partner as well.

## Hormonal Contraception: Effect on Labs

Oral contraceptives suppress the body's own axis: while on them, LH, FSH, estradiol, and
progesterone reflect the medication, not ovarian function. Assessing ovarian reserve and
sex hormones while on oral contraceptives is of little value. Oral contraceptives also
raise SHBG (lowering free testosterone – hence the therapeutic effect for
acne/hirsutism) and can affect thyroid markers and cortisol-binding globulin. This must
be considered when interpreting labs, and the user should not be alarmed by
"abnormalities" that are actually an expected effect of the medication.

## When to Refer Firmly to a Physician
- Amenorrhea (other than obvious pregnancy) and a newly emerged, persistently irregular
  cycle.
- A menopausal picture (high FSH, amenorrhea) in a woman younger than 40.
- Signs of hyperandrogenism with rapid progression or virilization.
- Galactorrhea (discharge from the breast outside lactation), especially with a cycle
  disturbance.
- Heavy, prolonged, or intermenstrual bleeding; any bleeding in postmenopause requires
  an in-person evaluation without delay.
- Suspected PCOS – for formal diagnosis and assessment of metabolic risks.
- Infertility or recurrent pregnancy loss – to a reproductive endocrinologist.
- Pregnancy and breastfeeding – any hormonal questions and nutraceutical use should be
  coordinated with a physician.
