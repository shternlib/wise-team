# Cortisol, Adrenal Glands, Circadian Rhythm

Applies to both sexes.

## Cortisol: Norms and Rhythm

Cortisol has a pronounced daily rhythm: a peak in the morning, a minimum in the late
evening/night. Therefore the timing of the test is critical.

- **Morning serum cortisol (7–9 AM):** approximately ~140–550 nmol/L (~5–20 µg/dL); the
  limits depend on the lab and method.
- **Late-evening/midnight cortisol:** normally much lower than morning – approximately
  less than ~140 nmol/L (~5 µg/dL) in serum. Late-night salivary cortisol is a sensitive
  marker: its elevation indicates loss of the normal rhythm (an important screen when
  hypercortisolism/Cushing's syndrome is suspected).
- **24-hour urinary free cortisol** and **salivary cortisol** (several points across the
  day, a "cortisol curve") reflect the real picture better than a single serum test.

"Adrenal fatigue" is not a medically recognized diagnosis. The real conditions are
adrenal insufficiency (cortisol deficiency) and hypercortisolism (excess). "Burnout"
symptoms are more often linked to chronic stress, sleep loss, circadian rhythm
disruption, depression, and deficiencies – not to "exhausted adrenals." This is important
to explain correctly to the user.

## Chronically Elevated Cortisol (Functional, Stress-Related)

Chronic stress keeps cortisol elevated or breaks its daily rhythm. Consequences:
- Abdominal (visceral) obesity.
- Insulin resistance, rising glucose.
- Suppression of sex hormones (testosterone in men, cycle disturbances in women,
  functional hypothalamic amenorrhea).
- Sleep disturbances (especially early awakenings, "alertness at the wrong time").
- Reduced immune function, frequent infections.
- Loss of muscle mass, slowed recovery.
- Increased blood pressure.
- Anxiety, irritability, "brain fog."

This is a functional condition and it responds well to lifestyle work. It is important to
distinguish it from true Cushing's syndrome (see below).

### Strategies to Normalize Cortisol and the Stress Response (may be recommended)
- **Sleep and circadian rhythm** – the foundation. A stable bedtime and wake time, a dark
  cool bedroom.
- **Bright morning light** for 10–30 minutes – it "sets" the normal daily rhythm of
  cortisol.
- **Meditation, breathing practices** (for example, an extended exhale, breathing at
  4–6 breaths per minute) – even 10 minutes/day measurably reduce the stress response.
- **Regular but not excessive exercise.** Excessive training volumes without recovery,
  conversely, raise cortisol.
- **Time in nature**, walks.
- **Social connection** – oxytocin and support buffer stress.
- **Limiting caffeine** in the second half of the day (a guide – no later than ~2 PM;
  caffeine raises cortisol and prolongs its effect).
- **Limiting alcohol** – it disrupts sleep and recovery architecture.

Nutraceuticals (an addition, not a substitute; coordinate with a physician in
pregnancy/lactation and with concurrent therapy):
- Adaptogens – ashwagandha (~300–600 mg/day of a standardized extract), rhodiola
  (~200–400 mg/day). The evidence base is moderate; there is an effect on perceived
  stress. Ashwagandha can affect the thyroid (it tends to raise thyroid hormones) – use
  caution with thyroid disease; it is not recommended in pregnancy.
- **Magnesium** (glycinate is well tolerated, ~200–400 mg in the evening) – for
  deficiency.
- **L-theanine** ~200 mg – reduces subjective stress without sedation.
- **Phosphatidylserine** ~300–400 mg – there is data on blunting the cortisol response to
  stress.

## DHEA-S

An adrenal androgen precursor, a marker of adrenal function.
- Declines with age (gradually after ~25–30 years).
- Very high DHEA-S is a reason to rule out adrenal hyperplasia or a tumor (especially
  with hyperandrogenism in women).
- Low DHEA-S – occurs in adrenal insufficiency, chronic stress, and glucocorticoid use.
- DHEA as a supplement is a hormonal agent; the skill does not recommend it as a
  nutraceutical – this is a question for a physician.

## Serious Adrenal Conditions (Referral to a Physician)

**Cushing's syndrome/disease (cortisol excess):** weight gain with redistribution (face,
abdomen, "hump"), purple wide striae, easy bruising, thinning skin, proximal muscle
weakness, rising blood pressure and glucose; in women – cycle disturbances and hirsutism.
A combination of these signs is a reason for evaluation by an endocrinologist.

**Adrenal insufficiency (cortisol deficiency, including Addison's disease):** marked
fatigue, weight loss, low blood pressure, skin darkening, salt craving, nausea,
dizziness on standing. This is a potentially dangerous condition; an acute adrenal crisis
is an emergency. It requires urgent medical attention.

## When to Refer Firmly to a Physician
- Signs of Cushing's syndrome (striae, fat redistribution, muscle weakness).
- Signs of adrenal insufficiency (hypotension, skin darkening, weight loss, salt
  craving) – urgently.
- A persistently disrupted daily cortisol rhythm in labs.
- Very high or very low DHEA-S.
- Any suspected adrenal crisis – emergency care.
